We assessed the epilepsy-YOD association in France and Sweden and YOD risk after midlife epilepsy. METHODS: We conducted 3 nationwide analyses. A French nested case-control analysis included patients with incident YOD aged 40-64 years, diagnosed between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2018. Index date was the first YOD-related health care event; controls were risk-set matched on age, sex, and region. A Swedish nested case-control replication included patients with YOD diagnosed between January 1, 1987, and December 31, 2016, with matched controls. In both studies, epilepsy-related diagnoses and antiseizure medication (ASM) dispensations were assessed up to 10 years before index date. Odds ratios (ORs) were estimated with conditional logistic regression. A French retrospective cohort included adults aged 30-55 years with incident epilepsy in 2008, matched to individuals with prevalent epilepsy before 2008 or no epilepsy. The cohort index was the first ASM reimbursement in the incident epilepsy group or the matched date for comparators. Follow-up ended at YOD, death, or 10 years. 摘要：One-third of the world's 70 million people with epilepsy have seizures uncontrolled by medication, and implantable devices are a promising treatment. These devices can detect impending seizures, missed medication and impaired cognition, but, like most medical devices, they cannot share this information with patients in real time. This is a missed opportunity for devices to learn from clinical events and teach patients how their behaviors affect their health. Here we present a device platform that converses with patients to help them manage their epilepsy. The inpatient prototype links scalp and intracranial electroencephalography (EEG) to a secure, large language model-based artificial intelligence (AI) that communicates bidirectionally through a smartphone app. The AI processes biomarkers of sleep, medication, cognition and seizure probability, notifies patients of clinical events and trends, records symptoms and responses, and uses their feedback to retrain its seizure detector, reducing false alarms. We validated this platform in 13 patients who are undergoing video-EEG monitoring for epilepsy (scalp, n = 11; intracranial, n = 2). In a pseudoprospective simulation, seizure detectors improved precision from patient feedback alone, without expert intervention. We propose this system as a scalable, safeguarded platform for medical devices that can rapidly adapt to patient and provider needs across epilepsy and other medical conditions.

摘要：The basal ganglia are an evolutionarily conserved neural circuit best known for the selection and sequencing of voluntary movement. Increasing evidence, however, indicates that these structures also play an important role in regulating pathological brain states, including seizures. Early pharmacological and lesion studies demonstrated that manipulation of basal ganglia output nuclei, particularly the substantia nigra pars reticulata, profoundly alters seizure susceptibility across multiple experimental models. Subsequent work expanded this view, revealing that several basal ganglia structures, including the striatum, subthalamic nucleus, globus pallidus, and superior colliculus, participate in a distributed network capable of modulating seizure initiation, propagation, and generalization. Modern systems neuroscience approaches have begun to define the specific cell types and projection-defined pathways through which these effects are mediated. Together, these findings support the concept that the basal ganglia function as a "seizure-gating" network that interacts with thalamocortical and brainstem arousal systems to regulate cortical synchronization. Understanding how these circuits influence seizure dynamics may enable new therapeutic strategies that strengthen endogenous seizure-suppressing networks rather than targeting the seizure focus alone. PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY: The basal ganglia are a group of deep brain structures best known for helping to start and stop movements. Over the past 40 years, research has shown that they also act as a brake on seizures. Studies across species demonstrate that "turning off" one of the main output structures of the basal ganglia, the substantia nigra pars reticulata, can stop many types of seizures. This review presents the "wiring diagram" of this brain circuit, including the effects of "turning on" and "turning off" individual brain regions and their connections on seizure initiation, propagation, and termination.
